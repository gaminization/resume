# How to Score 95+ on Resume Worded — What Their Own System Actually Checks

**Source note:** There's no public gallery of "top-scoring Resume Worded resumes" — scores are generated privately per upload, tied to an individual's account, and nobody posts the literal 95+ PDF alongside their score. What I found instead, and what this report is built from: **Resume Worded's own published scoring rubric** (their score-guide, scanner, and optimizer pages), their **actual before/after bullet rewrites** shown on their homepage and blog as worked examples, and independent reviews that used the real tool and reported what it flagged. This gets you the actual rubric rather than a guess at it.

---

## 1. How the score is actually built

- Scale: **0–100**, built from **20–30+ individual checks** (different pages on their own site cite "20+" and "30+" — likely the free Score tool runs ~20+ and the paid Optimizer runs the fuller 30+ set).
- The checks are grouped into (per their own current score-guide page) **three headline areas — Impact, Brevity, and Style** — with two more dimensions folded in across other pages: **Skills** and **ATS readability**. Practically, think of it as five buckets:
  1. **Impact** — quantified results, strong/correctly-tensed action verbs, accomplishment-oriented language (not duty-listing)
  2. **Brevity & wording** — length, bullet length, filler words, buzzwords/clichés, passive voice, personal pronouns, spelling
  3. **Style / growth signals** — leadership, teamwork, communication, analytical skills, and "drive" — signals of career growth across roles
  4. **Skills** — presence and relevance of the right keywords
  5. **ATS & formatting** — file readability, date-format consistency, punctuation consistency, page density, section structure, contact-detail placement
- **A perfect 10 on every criterion = 100/100.** The checks are explicitly described as **weighted by importance** and **adapted to your seniority level** — a check that matters for a new grad may be weighted differently for a 10-year veteran.
- **Score bands, per their own guide**: 85+ means you've cleared the mistakes a hiring manager spots in the first few seconds; 90+ means the resume "passes every check we run." A real user testimonial on their homepage: *"I got mine to 95. Got interviews and a job I am looking forward to."*
- **One AutoFix pass typically pushes an 85 to 90+**, per their own guide — AutoFix is their tool for auto-rewriting flagged bullets rather than making you do it manually.

## 2. The actual before/after rewrites they use to teach this (real examples from their site)

These are Resume Worded's own worked examples of exactly what a flagged line looks like before and after correction — this is the closest thing to seeing their rubric applied line by line:

| Flagged issue | Before | After (their fix) |
|---|---|---|
| No metrics | "Responsible for managing client accounts" | "Managed business relationships with 10 clients, including Volkswagen and Deutsche Bank; renewed 95% of contracts year over year" |
| Weak verb | "Helped with the team's marketing emails" | "Designed an email toolkit and style guide enabling teams to build mobile-responsive emails; saved 5+ hours per week" |
| Vague | "Worked on various team projects" | "Managed a 5-member cross-functional team and coordinated with six business partners to launch an e-commerce platform" |
| Already strong (no fix needed) | — | "Cut onboarding time from 6 weeks to 4" — flagged **strong ✓** as-is |

Notice the pattern in every fix: **specific number + specific scope (who/what) + a verb that names the actual action**, not a duty description. Their separate "resume synonyms" pages reinforce this for individual weak verbs — e.g., their page on the word "Contributed" recommends replacing it with something like **"Launched"** or **"Optimized"**, always paired with a metric: *"Launched five new products, catapulting profitability by 20% and gaining a 15% market share... within the first quarter."*

## 3. What their ATS scanner specifically checks for (the parsing layer, separate from wording)

Their scanner page describes literally showing you the parsed output of your own resume — i.e., what the ATS extracts — so you can compare it to what you intended. Specific failure modes they name from testing real resumes side by side:
- A **single-column resume parses in order**, top to bottom, correctly.
- A **sidebar column interleaves with the main experience column** — content gets jumbled together rather than read as two separate blocks.
- A **certifications table collapses into a single line** — individual certifications lose their separation once parsed.
- **Photos and skill-rating bars, because they're graphics and not text, are not extracted at all** — that content simply never reaches the recruiter's searchable database.

This matches (and independently confirms) the technical parsing findings from the broader ATS research done earlier — single column, no tables, no graphics.

## 4. Their own stated priority list for getting the score up (direct from the Resume Optimizer page)

Quoted close to verbatim because it's unusually blunt and specific for a vendor's own guidance: *"Delete the objective statement, buzzwords, and any bullet a stranger in your job could also claim. Keep bullets under two lines."*

Breaking that into concrete actions:
- **Cut the objective/summary statement** if it's generic — this is a direct, vendor-stated instruction, not just third-party advice.
- **Cut buzzwords and clichés** — their brevity check explicitly penalizes these.
- **The "stranger test"**: if a bullet is generic enough that a random other person in your same job title could also honestly claim it, it's not pulling its weight — it needs a specific number or a specific named outcome that's yours alone.
- **Two-line bullet ceiling** — anything longer gets flagged for brevity, regardless of how good the content is.

## 5. What the free tool does and doesn't do — an important limitation to know before optimizing around it

- The **free Score tool grades your resume in general**, against broad good-resume habits — it does **not** compare you against a specific job posting.
- **Job-specific keyword matching** is a separate, paid feature called **"Targeted Resume"** — this is where you'd get a true job-fit score against one specific posting; the free score is a general-quality score, not a job-match score.
- One independent competitor's review makes the limitation explicit: *"An ATS does not care whether your resume is well written in the abstract. It checks whether your resume matches the role in front of it."* — worth remembering that a 95 on the general free score is not the same claim as "this resume is optimized for Job X."
- An independent review (MobileAppDaily) rates the tool as well-suited specifically for **early-to-mid career, ATS/recruiter-readability optimization** — and explicitly recommends senior professionals or creative-field candidates pair it with human judgment rather than chase the number alone.

## Cross-source synthesis — the actual checklist to hit 95+

1. **Every bullet needs a number and a named scope** — this is the single most repeated pattern across every one of their own worked examples. Not "improved X" — "improved X by Y% for Z people/clients/systems."
2. **Kill "Assisted," "Helped," "Responsible for," "Worked on," "Contributed to"** — these are named, specifically, as automatically-flagged weak-verb triggers.
3. **Cut the objective statement and any generic, stranger-claimable bullet** — this is Resume Worded's own stated first move, not a generic tip.
4. **Two lines per bullet, maximum.**
5. **Single column, no tables, no graphics/photos/skill bars** — anything not extracted as plain text can't be scored on content at all, since the parser never sees it.
6. **Remember what the free score actually measures**: general resume quality (Impact/Brevity/Style/Skills/ATS), not fit to a specific job posting — a 95 here is not the same signal as a high match score against one specific job description, which is a separate paid feature.
7. **One AutoFix pass is explicitly described as usually enough to jump from 85 to 90+** — the remaining climb to 95+ is described as fine-tuning: tightening a couple of bullets, adding one more missing metric, swapping one more soft verb — not a structural overhaul.
